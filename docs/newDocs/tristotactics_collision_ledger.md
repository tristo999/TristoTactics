# TristoTactics — Collision Ledger

The actual design blueprint. This replaces the old 21-beat story bible as the
thing you build from. A collision is a scene that happens twice — once in Act 1
under the lie, once in Act 2 from the other side. Each row is a small machine
for breaking someone's heart on purpose.

Last updated: 2026-05-31


## How to read this

Four columns per collision:

- **Act 1 surface** — what the player sees and *feels* the first time, under the
  lie. Must be cheap, sincere, and unmarked. The player spends a real emotion
  here (triumph, contempt, dismissal) without knowing it's a deposit.
- **Act 2 truth** — what the scene actually was. The withdrawal, with interest.
- **Cost to land it** — what the Act 2 player pays, plays, or chooses to make
  the scene land. This is the column the old docs never had, and it's where the
  mercy-under-constraint gameplay lives.
- **Retrieval cue** — the sensory fingerprint planted in Act 1 so the player can
  *recover* the first version when the second aligns. Without this, the late
  recognition is a hope. With it, it's engineered.

It's missing a *kindness that betrayed.* That's the nastiest row and its absence
is real.


## Craft notes per row

### Row 2 — the unheard face-off (the arc)

Don't play this once. It's a three-stage progression across Act 2, and each
stage must feel different or it reads as the same scene three times:

1. **Bargaining.** Early. The party believes, against what they should know,
   that *this* time they can get through. Hoods down, own voices, shouting. It
   fails — the filter holds, the friend's face resolves them into a monster.
   The failure costs. Someone says the quiet thing afterward: *they can't hear
   us. They were never going to. We didn't hear us.* You get this beat once.

2. **Refusing to stop anyway.** The middle, the most human part. They *know*
   now. The bargaining is over. And they still try — but it shifts from
   "convince" to "reach," from words to the Guardian's power, from strategy to
   something closer to prayer. This is where it hurts most: not hope, love that
   won't quit knowing it's futile. The cost rises — reaching backward with the
   fragment drains the Act 2 hero, leaves them vulnerable mid-fight. The
   mechanic carries the meaning: reaching backward weakens you here and now.

3. **The one that lands.** The hero pushes a single fragment through — not a
   sentence (nothing completes until the capital), a word, a wrong color. It
   surfaces in Act 1 as a glitch the hero already lived through and dismissed as
   artifact bleed. **It works, and it changes nothing.** The triumph and the
   futility are the same event. Do not soften this. If pushing through altered
   Act 1, the loop collapses and the whole game's logic is a lie. The reward for
   the impossible is confirmation that even success can't bend the silhouette.

Build stage 1 scripted (to teach the language). Hand the player the verb for
stages 2–3 so the futility is something they *enact*, not witness. Consider
making "reach back" an actual Act 2 move — a button whose entire function is to
attempt the impossible at a price. It's not a good mechanic by genre standards;
it doesn't help you win. It's a grief mechanic. It might be the most
TristoTactics thing in the game.

**The buried gift:** because the hero can push fragments back, *some* of what
the Act 1 hero is chasing all game — "a voice that asked them to find it" — is
partly their own future self, reaching back. The call comes from inside the
loop. Never stated. A second-playthrough gut-punch the characters never name.

### Row 3 — the comrade you celebrated killing

The decision that makes this devastating instead of merely sad: **does the Act 2
party know, going in, that the boss will be their comrade?**

- *They know* → planned sacrifice, the comrade volunteers. Clean. Risks a
  heroic speech, which is exactly the cliché this project avoids.
- *They don't* → the comrade peels off as a tactical call, no idea they're about
  to become the boss the hero already beat. The identity lands on the party and
  the player together, after, in the quiet. No speech, no foreknowledge, just
  grief. **This matches the tone.** Recommended.

Consider not even showing the Act 2 kill — keep the player on the objective, let
it happen off-screen, reported by absence. The player already *saw* it in Act 1
from the winning side with a victory screen. The Act 1 footage is the death
scene; Act 2 just hands you the caption.

**Non-negotiable:** the Act 1 victory must be genuinely unremarkable and
genuinely celebrated. No ominous swell, no lingering camera, no companion saying
"something felt off about that one." The forgettability is the weapon. The
player has to have genuinely not cared, so Act 2 can make them care
retroactively about someone they didn't bother to look at. Every designer
instinct is to *mark* important moments; this one's power is in being unmarked.


### Row 5 — the camp you defended

The Act 2 interior is rich: the party slips through the emptied camp while the
Act 1 hero fights outside, and **overhears Vael with his mask off** — bored
contempt for the kingdom's "pathetic" defense, an offhand aside dismissing the
incoming hero and companions as "a band of misfits" — cut off mid-sentence by
the flash of the summoning. They watch his warmth switch back on as he leaves to
greet the hero. The clock is the Act 1 battle itself; when the fighting stops,
the window closes. **Non-negotiable, same as Row 3:** the Act 1 skirmish must
read as a forgettable win — no ominous scoring, no companion saying "that felt
off" — so Act 2 can reveal you were the distraction in your own heist.

## Rules for the whole system

1. **Collisions are peaks, not texture.** Act 2 is mostly its own campaign with
   its own objectives. If the timelines constantly bump, the device gets cheap
   and exhausting. The power is in the gap. The breathing room is what lets the
   player put the dread down — which is the state you want them in when the next
   collision hits.

2. **Never announce the repeat.** Act 2 collision scenes play as their own
   scenes on their own terms. No "remember this?" No flashback intercut at the
   moment of impact.

3. **Recognition is the player's, near the end, all at once.** The pieces align
   late and the player *realizes* they've been replaying Act 1 blind. Hand them
   the final piece, then go quiet. If the game does the connecting, it steals the
   complicity, which is the whole point. No montage confession. The ideal: the
   player whispers "oh my god, the bridge— the commander—" a beat *before* the
   game would ever say it.

4. **Retrieval cues are mandatory, sensory, and unmarked.** Every collision
   needs a fingerprint planted in Act 1: forgettable enough not to brace for,
   recoverable at the click. Plant the *language* of cues early and cheaply
   (e.g. a low-stakes garbled-figure encounter the player dismisses) so the
   filter is already fluent before it matters. Establish the mechanic as
   tragedy, not as a twist sprung at the payoff.

5. **The loop is fixed outside, authored inside.** The silhouette of every
   collision is locked — the bridge falls, the boss dies, the figure goes
   un-heard. The interior — how, why, what it cost — is yours. Authoring an
   interior never moves a silhouette.

6. **Mercy minimizes cost everywhere except the finale.** Each collision is a
   chance to do the inevitable *better* than the first time. The finale is the
   one inversion, where you must finally spend lives and grace becomes being
   worth it.


## Methodology — how to build with this

**Design backward from the collisions.** Figure out every "oh shit" first, then
go plant its innocent version in Act 1 so it's sitting there, forgotten,
waiting. You can't fully write Act 1 until you know what Act 2 will bill it for.
The acts are designed from the collisions outward, not front to back.

**The ledger is the spine; the rest is connective tissue.** Don't grow a
21-beat bible again. Grow the ledger. When a new "happens twice" idea arrives,
it gets a row, all four columns. If you can't fill the cost column or the cue
column, the collision isn't finished — those two columns are where the design
actually lives.

**Probably few rows, each airtight.** Devastation doesn't stack linearly. Five
perfect gut-punches beat twelve decent ones. Six or seven collisions, each
airtight, connected by lighter material, is a solo-dev-sized game — and a
finishable one.
